Solver technical detail

Under the Hood

The air the bullet flies through, and how Bullseye turns live wind into a hold.

Our approach

No shortcuts in the math

You’ll hear it at every range: You can’t shoot the difference. And there’s truth in it. Your wind call, your position and your trigger will always matter more than the third decimal place of a drag curve.

But that’s a point about the shooter, not the solver.

Small effects don’t stay small. They add up: the air thinning as a bullet climbs, spin drift, Coriolis, aerodynamic jump, drag that changes every millisecond of the flight. Any one of them might be “too small to shoot.” Together, at distance, they’re clicks.

So Bullseye doesn’t decide for you which physics is small enough to skip. It runs the full calculation every time and gives you the most precise answer it can. What you do with it is up to you.

The solver should never be the reason you missed.

Under the hood · Angled shots

The bullet climbs into thinner air

Shoot uphill and the bullet doesn't stay in the air it left the muzzle in. On a +20° shot to 1,000 yards, the target sits about 312 m (1,025 ft) above you, where the air is roughly 3% thinner. Thinner air means less drag, so the bullet holds more speed and drops less.

Bullseye updates air density throughout the flight as the bullet gains or loses height, instead of assuming muzzle conditions the whole way. It works in both directions: shooting downhill, the bullet descends into denser air and the solver accounts for that too.

Bullet path curvature is exaggerated for clarity. Heights and distances are to scale.

Under the hood · Wind

How Bullseye turns live wind into a hold

In short: we take the last few seconds of sensor wind, weight it toward the newest readings, and smooth it more at longer range. That wind goes into a full flight simulation, and the sideways drift it computes becomes your hold.

1Smoothing the live wind reading

Raw wind data is noisy. If your hold jumped with every flicker of the meter, you'd be chasing numbers instead of shooting. So Bullseye smooths live sensor wind with an exponential moving average (EMA).

  • Weighted toward what's happening now. New readings count the most. Older readings never drop out completely; they just count for less and less. In practice, your hold reflects mostly the last 3 to 5 seconds of wind.
  • Matched to your target distance. A bullet headed to 1,000 yards is in the air much longer than one headed to 300, so it lives through more of the wind. Bullseye smooths more at longer distances, using user tunable distance bands chosen to roughly match bullet flight time.
  • Direction is averaged correctly. Wind direction is averaged as a vector, not as raw compass degrees. A wind swinging between 350° and 10° averages to about 0°, as it should, not 180°, which is what a simple average of the numbers would give you.

2You still see every gust

Only the hold is smoothed. The Wind number on the solution card shows the raw sensor reading, so you see a gust the moment it arrives and can decide for yourself whether to wait it out or send it.

3You stay in control

  • Manual wind is never touched. Only live sensor wind is smoothed. Wind you type in by hand goes into the solve exactly as entered.
  • Adjustable. You can change the smoothing level for each distance band, or turn off distance matching entirely, in Settings → Sensors and GPS → Wind smoothing.

4From wind to hold: a full trajectory simulation

Bullseye doesn't use a shortcut formula for wind. The smoothed wind goes into the same full trajectory solver that computes your elevation:

  1. Wind is converted to a clock direction relative to your direction of fire and treated as constant across the whole flight.
  2. The bullet is simulated in 1-millisecond steps from muzzle to target. Flight time is built into the physics, not estimated.
  3. Drag is applied to the bullet's speed through the air, not over the ground. That's how real air resistance works, and it's why a crosswind keeps pushing the bullet sideways for the entire flight.
  4. The hold is calculated. The bullet's sideways distance at the target, plus spin drift and your zero offset, is divided by the range to give your hold in mil or MOA.
  5. Aerodynamic jump goes to elevation. A crosswind also nudges the bullet slightly up or down, an effect called aerodynamic jump. Bullseye applies it to your elevation, not your wind hold, because it moves the bullet vertically.

Under the hood · Elevation

How your elevation hold is worked out

You zero once. Bullseye works out how far your bore is tilted up from your scope’s line of sight at zero, then flies today’s shot at that tilt through today’s air, one millisecond at a time. The hold keeps up as your rifle changes: the weather, the powder temperature, a cold or clean bore, and the barrel's round count.

1Zero once, shoot anywhere

Zero at 1,000 ft, then hunt at 6,000 ft in different weather. You don't re-zero: Bullseye already knows what your zero means.

Your scope sits above the bore, so to meet the line of sight at your zero distance, the bore points slightly up. That angle is your zero, and once the scope is set the air can't change it. Bullseye finds it the way you would at the range: it fires a test shot, sees whether it lands high or low at your zero distance, and adjusts until it lands exactly on your zero.

That search runs in the conditions of the day you zeroed:

  • the temperature, pressure and humidity you zeroed in
  • your muzzle velocity that day
  • no wind, no uphill or downhill angle, no Earth spin
  • your zero offset, if your rifle groups a little high or low at zero

Then it flies that same angle through the air where you are now. Enter your zero-day weather once in your rifle's setup. When you arrive somewhere new, fire a check group to confirm the scope didn't move on the trip.

2Today's shot

Bullseye launches the bullet at your zero angle and follows it to the target in 1-millisecond steps. At every step it adds up what acts on the bullet:

  • Gravity pulls it down.
  • Drag slows it. Drag comes from today's air density (temperature, pressure and humidity), how fast the bullet is going compared with the speed of sound, and your bullet's G1 or G7 drag curve and BC.
  • Shot angle: uphill or downhill, less of gravity acts across your line of sight, so the hold gets smaller. Bullseye works this out in the full flight, not with the rifleman's rule.

No shortcut formula is used. Drag slows the bullet's fall as well as its forward speed, and only a full flight captures that.

3Velocity that follows your rifle

At long range, muzzle velocity is one of the biggest drivers of your elevation. Bullseye picks the velocity for this shot, not a single number from the box:

  • Powder temperature. Enter your chronograph results at two or more temperatures, and the velocity follows the powder from a cold morning to a hot afternoon.
  • Cold bore. Enter your cold-bore velocity, and your first shot of the day gets its own hold.
  • Clean bore. After a cleaning, the first shots use your clean-bore velocity.
  • Barrel wear. Log velocity checks as your barrel's round count climbs. Once three or more checks show a real trend and you turn on Adjust MV for wear, the velocity follows the count, and the solution says so: "MV −12 fps for barrel wear at 1,240 rounds." It doesn't stretch the trend far past your checks: once you've shot past them, it asks for a new one.

4Truing that fixes the right number

When your real dope doesn't match the solution, Bullseye trues it from two distances. Enter the elevation that actually hit at a middle distance and a far one, and it solves for a velocity correction and a drag correction together.

Why two: velocity and drag bend the curve in different ways. True from one distance and velocity has to absorb a BC error, so the hold is right at that distance and off at the others. With two, each correction takes the error it should, and the whole curve comes out right. Your bullet's published BC and your measured velocity stay saved as you entered them.

5The small effects, counted for you

Two effects worth a few inches at 1,000 yd are in every solve. Bullseye works them out from your twist, your wind and your location.

Aerodynamic jump. A crosswind moves your shot up or down as well as sideways. As the bullet leaves the muzzle, the crosswind pushes on its nose. A spinning bullet acts like a gyroscope, so instead of turning downwind, the nose tips straight up or straight down, and keeps that small angle all the way to the target.

Its size depends on your bullet's stability (Sg) and its length. A typical match bullet in a full 10 mph crosswind moves about 0.4 MOA, roughly 4 in at 1,000 yd.

The Eötvös effect. The Earth turns under the bullet while it flies. Fire east and the bullet travels with the Earth's spin, so it falls a little less and hits high. Fire west and it hits low. Due north or south, there's no effect. At 40° N, a 1,000 yd shot due east lands about 3 in high, or 0.3 MOA. The sideways part of the Earth's spin, Coriolis, goes into your windage hold. Enter your latitude (GPS can fill it in) and your direction of fire, and both are counted.

6From drop to hold

At the target distance, Bullseye measures how far below your line of sight the bullet is, aerodynamic jump and the Eötvös effect included. The hold is that drop as an angle, in mil or MOA. Spin drift moves the bullet sideways only, so it's in your windage hold, not here.

Bullseye also tells you what limits the shot. Your hit chance comes with an error budget: how much of the spread in your impacts comes from each source, with the largest given in mil or MOA. The sources are your wind read, velocity spread, range estimate, BC and your own group size. For elevation at long range, the biggest is usually your velocity spread or your range estimate, so you know what to work on next.
