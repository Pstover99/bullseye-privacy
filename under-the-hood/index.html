Under the Hood

Why an angled shot changes the air the bullet flies through, and how Bullseye turns live wind into a hold.

Under the hood · Angled shots

The bullet climbs into thinner air

Shoot uphill and the bullet doesn't stay in the air it left the muzzle in. On a +20° shot to 1,000 yards, the target sits about 312 m (1,025 ft) above you, where the air is roughly 3% thinner. Thinner air means less drag, so the bullet holds more speed and drops less.

Bullseye updates air density throughout the flight as the bullet gains or loses height, instead of assuming muzzle conditions the whole way. It works in both directions: shooting downhill, the bullet descends into denser air and the solver accounts for that too.

Bullet path curvature is exaggerated for clarity. Heights and distances are to scale.

Under the hood · Wind

How Bullseye turns live wind into a hold

In short: we take the last few seconds of sensor wind, weight it toward the newest readings, and smooth it more at longer range. That wind goes into a full flight simulation, and the sideways drift it computes becomes your hold.

1Smoothing the live wind reading

Raw wind data is noisy. If your hold jumped with every flicker of the meter, you'd be chasing numbers instead of shooting. So Bullseye smooths live sensor wind with an exponential moving average (EMA).

  • Weighted toward what's happening now. New readings count the most. Older readings never drop out completely; they just count for less and less. In practice, your hold reflects mostly the last 3 to 5 seconds of wind.
  • Matched to your target distance. A bullet headed to 1,000 yards is in the air much longer than one headed to 300, so it lives through more of the wind. Bullseye smooths more at longer distances, using distance bands chosen to roughly match bullet flight time.
  • Direction is averaged correctly. Wind direction is averaged as a vector, not as raw compass degrees. A wind swinging between 350° and 10° averages to about 0°, as it should, not 180°, which is what a simple average of the numbers would give you.

2You still see every gust

Only the hold is smoothed. The Wind number on the solution card shows the raw sensor reading, so you see a gust the moment it arrives and can decide for yourself whether to wait it out or send it.

3You stay in control

  • Manual wind is never touched. Only live sensor wind is smoothed. Wind you type in by hand goes into the solve exactly as entered.
  • Adjustable. You can change the smoothing level for each distance band, or turn off distance matching entirely, in Settings → Sensors and GPS → Wind smoothing.

4From wind to hold: a full trajectory simulation

Bullseye doesn't use a shortcut formula for wind. The smoothed wind goes into the same full trajectory solver that computes your elevation:

  1. Wind is converted to a clock direction relative to your direction of fire and treated as constant across the whole flight.
  2. The bullet is simulated in 1-millisecond steps from muzzle to target. Flight time is built into the physics, not estimated.
  3. Drag is applied to the bullet's speed through the air, not over the ground. That's how real air resistance works, and it's why a crosswind keeps pushing the bullet sideways for the entire flight.
  4. The hold is calculated. The bullet's sideways distance at the target, plus spin drift and your zero offset, is divided by the range to give your hold in mil or MOA.
  5. Aerodynamic jump goes to elevation. A crosswind also nudges the bullet slightly up or down, an effect called aerodynamic jump. Bullseye applies it to your elevation, not your wind hold, because it moves the bullet vertically.
