Learn to read the wind

See the wind, then learn to read it

A wind meter at the bench tells you what the wind is doing at the bench. Your bullet spends most of its flight somewhere else.

Reading the wind is the hardest skill in long-range shooting, and the slowest to learn. The usual way is to make a call, take the shot, and work backward from where it landed. You almost never know what the wind was really doing at 500 or 900 yards, so every lesson is partly a guess, and getting good can take years of range time.

Wind Corridor was built to shorten that. It shows you what the wind is doing along the whole shot while you're looking at it, so every session at the range teaches you something real.

Place wireless wind meters downrange. Bullseye Ballistics then shows the wind along the entire shot, live, laid over satellite imagery of your own range.

How you learn

Pick a meter at 900 yards and read its speed and direction. Then look at the mirage, the leaves, the trees and the grass near 900 yards. That is what that wind looks like at that distance. Do it often enough and you learn to read the wind from the mirage, leaves, trees and grass alone, at any distance and on any range, with or without meters.

  • See it at every distance

    Live wind at every meter, shown at its real distance and updated about once a second, so what you see downrange has a reading to match.

  • Check your call

    Make your own read, then compare it with one net wind call for the whole shot: crosswind, direction, and head or tail.

  • Learn it on your range

    Your actual range, to scale, from public satellite imagery of your exact location, so every reading sits on ground you can see.

  • Learn from real readings

    Bad data is caught, and you always know what's measured and what's estimated.

Terrain and trees

See how the land shapes the wind

Wind doesn't cross a range evenly. Wind Corridor models how terrain, trees and dense forest change the natural flow of the wind, and draws it right on the map. The wind runs faster across open ground, slows when it reaches trees, and slows further in dense woods. The terrain bends it too, and you can watch it happen.

  • Open ground

    Wind moves fastest across open landscape.

  • Trees

    It slows when it meets a line of trees.

  • Dense woods

    It slows further still in thick forest.

This is what you're learning to read. When the flag in the clearing says one thing and the treeline says another, you can see why, and where along the shot the wind changes.

Why shear matters

Shear is the lesson a single bench meter can never teach you: the wind at your feet can be blowing the opposite way from the wind at 600 yards. Wind Corridor shows you where it changes, so you can find that change in the mirage and the trees.

Net wind, three ways

Switch how the Net Wind is calculated and compare three answers for the same wind: the firing-line meter alone, a simple average of every meter, and our ballistic calculation for your specific load. Spoiler: they're not even close. Averaging the wind you see downrange is not the same as working out the wind your bullet feels.

Meters

Meters that set themselves up

Setup takes minutes, not menus, so your time on the range goes into reading the wind. Meters find you, and you tell the app where they are.

  • Long-range downrange meters. Calypso ULP LoRa wind meters send their readings by long-range radio to a single receiver at the firing line. The receiver passes them to Bullseye Ballistics over Bluetooth.
  • They show up on their own. Each meter appears as soon as it's heard. No pairing each one, no typing in IDs. Enter its distance and it's in the picture.
  • Add a meter at the line. Connect a Calypso ultrasonic Bluetooth meter at the firing line, and it becomes your 0-yard reading, with battery level shown.
  • No exact aiming required. Meters are assumed to face the same way you're shooting. If one doesn't, type its heading in true or magnetic north and the readings are corrected for you.
  • Turn any meter on or off with the "Use in corridor" switch, without walking out to it.
Line of fire

Match the map to what you see

Stand at the firing line, point your phone at the target, and tap Capture. Your GPS position and direction of fire are recorded, in both magnetic and true north, so the corridor on screen lines up with the ground in front of you.

Fine-tune the direction 1° at a time, or type it in. Set your target distance by typing it or with quick ±50-yard buttons.

Data quality

Learn from data you can trust

One bad reading can teach you the wrong lesson. So every reading is checked before it reaches the screen, and you can see exactly what was held back and why.

  • Spike filter. A sudden jump of more than 3 m/s (about 6.7 mph) or 45° is held until the next readings confirm it. On by default, and you can switch it off.
  • Impossible readings rejected. A garbled radio packet claiming 265 mph never makes it into your numbers.
  • Rejected-readings log. Lists each held or rejected reading, with the time, meter, value and reason.
  • Link health at a glance. Receiver connection quality, dropouts, and automatic reconnects. For each meter: a LIVE badge, packets received in the last 30 seconds, the longest gap, and when it was last heard.
  • Radio timing check. Meters transmit on their own timing. If two drift close enough to interfere, you get a warning telling you which meter to power-cycle. It's a heads-up, never a roadblock.
  • Mode history. Shows when the view switched between no data, one meter and multiple meters, and why.
  • Record a session. Log everything your meters report, so you can go back over what the wind did after you leave the range.
Display

Make the wind easy to see

Tune the display to your eye and your conditions.

  • Three looks: Trails, Fog or Streaks.
  • Particle density and speed: a busier or calmer picture, and faster or slower motion.
  • Gust swirl: shows the air churning as gusts come through.
  • Show or hide the assumed-zone shading, the meter badges, and the satellite photo.
  • Photo dimming: fade the satellite photo so the wind stands out.
  • Your units: yards or meters; mph, km/h, m/s or knots.

01 Lesson 1

Same wind, three answers

The same three meters give three crosswinds on one card: about 8 mph from the firing-line meter alone, about 1.5 mph by simple average, and about 3 mph by Advanced Ballistics. All three say "from the left". One says hold for a strong wind, one says barely hold, and the ballistic number sits in between.

  • Shear: "crosswind changes side between 400 and 800". The wind pushes left at the bench and right downrange.
  • Head/tail: 2.4 to 3.4 mph tailwind, weighted the same way as the crosswind.
  • Coverage: "92% of the wind effect measured". With the method on Simple average, the same meters read "78% measured". Simple average counts yards: 224 of the 1,024 yd are unmeasured, so 22% is missing. Advanced Ballistics counts influence, and those last 224 yd sit near the target, where wind barely moves the bullet. Only 8% of the wind's effect is missing.
  • Gusts: peak 3.5–3.7 mph. Gusts are measured on the selected crosswind. The morning Simple average recordings peaked at about 1.1–1.4 mph. Part of the jump is the stronger wind later in the day, and part may be that the ballistic number leans on the bench wind, so its swings carry through more than they do in an average. Because the wind changed between recordings, these clips can't separate the two.
The lesson

The simple average lets the left wind at the bench and the right wind at 800 yd cancel each other out. A bullet doesn't work that way. The left wind sits right at the muzzle, where it pushes the bullet for its entire flight. The right wind sits near the target, where it has little time to act. So the left wind wins, and the hold is far bigger than the average suggests.

A single bench meter misleads the other way. Firing line only says 7.6–8.7 mph full value, two to three times what the bullet actually feels, because the wind farther out is weaker and turns to the other side.

02 Lesson 2

Why wind near the muzzle matters most

Tap Why different? on the comparison card and a sheet answers the question with two charts.

  • All three answers, side by side. In the clip: Advanced Ballistics 2.6–3.6 mph from the left (outlined in gold, because it's the active method), Simple average 0.9–1.9 mph, and Firing line only (0 yd) 7.5–8.7 mph, all from the left.
  • Influence by distance. A curve from 100% at the muzzle down to 0% at the target. It shows how much a given wind at each distance moves a 6.5 Creedmoor 140-grain bullet at 2,700 fps.
  • Your meters on the curve. Green markers sit at 0, 400 and 800 yd. The 0 yd meter counts fully, the 400 yd meter roughly 85%, and the 800 yd meter only about 45%.
  • Crosswind along the shot. The white line is the wind the meters measure along the line of fire: about 9 mph from the left at the muzzle, easing toward 400 yd, crossing to the right between 400 and 800 yd, and about 5 mph from the right at 800 yd. Past the last meter it's assumed to stay the same. The three flat lines are the three answers: blue dashes for Firing line only, orange for Advanced Ballistics, white dots for Simple average.
  • The reason in plain words: wind near the muzzle pushes the bullet for its whole remaining flight; wind near the target has almost no time to move it. And, new on the sheet: "Firing line only assumes the wind at the bench blows unchanged all the way to the target. When the wind changes along the shot, that assumption fails."
The lesson

The curve isn't a straight line. It stays high through the first half of the shot and drops steeply near the target. The bullet is still fast early on, but every early push has the rest of the flight to grow. Late wind, however strong, has run out of time.

The crosswind chart shows why both shortcuts miss. Firing line only draws a flat line at the bench wind, as if nothing changes downrange. Simple average draws a flat line through the middle, as if the 800 yd wind mattered as much as the bench wind. The orange Advanced Ballistics line sits between them, closer to the bench wind, because that's where the wind moves the bullet most.

For meter placement, the first few hundred yards deserve the most attention. A meter near the target tells you the least about the hold.

03 Lesson 3

Watching the wind follow the terrain

See the Wind draws the moving air over a satellite photo of the actual range, firing line at the bottom and target at the top. It's the fastest way to understand a wind call before reading a single number. In this clip the shear zone is circled, with a question for you: what would you see there in real life?

  • The wind turns along the shot. At the bench the streaks push left to right (6.5–6.9 mph from 8:30–9 o'clock). By 400 yd they swing toward a quartering tailwind (from 7:30). By 800 yd they run right to left (from 3–3:30). You can see the flip without reading any numbers.
  • Contour lines. White lines mark every 100 ft of elevation, so the hills and the low ground between them show on the photo.
  • "Modeled from terrain." Between the meters, the wind is shaped by the ground and trees, using USGS 3DEP elevation data, rather than blended in a straight line. Air bends around high ground and channels through low ground, and the streaks show it.
  • "Assumed · from 800 yd." Past the last meter the app has no measurement, so it carries the 800 yd reading out to the target and labels it.
  • The NET WIND bar at the top, here NET WIND · AVG: 1.0 mph ← LEFT · 1.6 tail · MULTI-POINT, shows the selected method and whether it comes from one meter or several.
The lesson

Wind isn't one arrow. Over 1,000 yards of hills and tree lines it turns, speeds up and slows down. The meters give exact readings at three points, the terrain model fills in the picture between them, and the labels always tell you which is which.

Reading the badges. Each meter shows its speed and the clock direction the wind comes from: 12 is a headwind, 6 a tailwind, 9 from the left, 3 from the right.

04 Lesson 4

Line of fire and the ground under the shot

Before wind can be split into crosswind and head/tail, the app needs to know exactly which way you're shooting, and how far.

  • Sensors. The LoRa receiver, relaying the two downrange meters, and the ultrasonic meter at the firing line, both connected, with receiver drops and Bluetooth timing shown.
  • Target. 1,024 yd, set by typing or with ±50 yd buttons.
  • Capture. Point the phone at the target and tap Capture. The app records the GPS position (±12 m here) and the compass direction: 194.9° magnetic = 184.9° true. That 10° difference is the local magnetic declination, which the app handles for you.
  • Nudge. ±1° buttons, or type the direction, to line it up exactly.
  • Ground card (LiDAR, 1 m). A small side view of the ground along the shot, with the line of sight drawn as a dashed line. Here the target is 207 ft lower than the shooter, 3.9° down, and the ground dips into a hollow, so the shot passes as high as 51 ft above the ground at 476 yd. Tap Path Profile for the full view.
The lesson

On a hill-to-hill shot, the bullet flies far above the meters. At 476 yd it's about 51 ft above the ground, not far past meter 1 at 400 yd. A meter on a 10 ft mast reads the wind near the ground, about 40 ft below the bullet, and wind usually blows harder higher up. So on ranges like this, the measured wind can understate what the bullet actually meets.

The profile makes that visible, and it's the first step toward learning, from logged shots, how much stronger the wind runs at shot height. LiDAR ground clearance, further down this page, maps that height along the whole shot and shows where a meter will read the air your bullet actually passes through.

Meters you can trust

A wind call is only as good as its readings. This part of the app shows each meter's health and every reading it refused to use.

The lesson

When the wind changes sides along the shot, a simple average can nearly cancel itself out and tell you to hold almost nothing. The bullet doesn't experience it that way. The bench wind pushes it for its whole flight, and the opposite wind at 800 yd only acts on the last part of the shot, when there's little time left to move it.

Hold for about 3 mph here, not the 0.7 mph average or the 7.4 mph bench reading. On the range, look for this pattern in the grass, leaves and mirage: flags or trees leaning one way near you and the other way downrange.

The Corridor card and "Why different?"

  • Corridor card: crosswind 2.8–3.0 mph left to right, a 3.1 mph gust, and shear of about 11 mph, because the wind reverses between 400 and 800 yd. Coverage is 92%.
  • All three answers: Advanced Ballistics 2.6–3.1 mph, Simple average 0.5–1.0 mph and Firing line only 7.1–7.7 mph, all left to right.
  • Crosswind along the bullet path: the line starts high at the muzzle, falls through 400 yd and crosses zero between 400 and 800 yd, where the wind changes sides.
  • Influence by distance: the same curve as Lesson 2. Wind near the muzzle bends the bullet's path for the full flight, and wind downrange acts for less time.

Meter Placement

Put your meters where they count

Meter Placement searches the whole satellite photo, not just the line of fire, for spots where a meter on a mast sits in the same air as your bullet.

  • Up to three recommended spots, numbered on the map. Tap one to see why it was chosen, for example 218 yd · 124 yd left · LiDAR shows the shot 31 ft above ground here · high influence · clear radio path.
  • Wind that matters most comes first. Spots near the muzzle score higher, because wind there moves the bullet the most.
  • A clear radio path back to your receiver for every spot, checked against the terrain. Move the receiver on the photo and the recommendations follow.
  • See what each spot adds. For example 3 meters · shot's air measured over 45% of the path. With recommended spots: 49%.
  • Choose one, two or three spots, depending on how many meters you have.

Settings that match how you shoot

  • Shooting position: prone (1 ft), sitting (3 ft) or bench (4 ft), or type your own height.
  • Target height, meter mast height and receiver height.
  • Trees: tree heights are taken into account where tree-height data is available, with an optional minimum allowance of your own.
  • Instant updates. Change a setting and the clearance and recommendations update immediately, with nothing new to download. Switch from prone to bench and max clearance goes from 51 ft to 53 ft.
Honest by design

You always know what the data is

  • LiDAR means LiDAR

    "LiDAR" appears only where the terrain data really is LiDAR (1 m). Elsewhere the app says "terrain data" and shows the true resolution.

  • Line of sight, not the arc

    Clearance is drawn along your line of sight. The real bullet arcs a little higher mid-range, and the app says so.

  • The photo isn't LiDAR

    The satellite photo is aerial photography. The LiDAR is the shape of the ground underneath it.

Getting started

What you need and how to start

What you need

  • Calypso ULP LoRa wind meters for downrange
  • A Calypso LoRa receiver at the firing line
  • Optional A Calypso ultrasonic Bluetooth wind meter at the firing line
  • Bullseye Ballistics on your phone

Setup

  1. Place the meters downrange along your line of fire, facing the same way you're shooting.
  2. Power on the meters and the receiver, then connect to the receiver from Bullseye Ballistics.
  3. As each meter appears, enter its distance.
  4. Optionally, connect your firing-line meter.
  5. Stand at the firing line, point your phone at the target, tap Capture, and enter your target distance.
  6. Read the wind downrange, make your call, then check it against the screen.
