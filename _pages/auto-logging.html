Why the record of a single shot matters

Long-range accuracy is an exercise in error budgeting. Every shot that lands is the sum of a firing solution and a set of departures from it: a muzzle velocity a few feet per second off its average, a wind call that missed by half a mile per hour, a density altitude that drifted with the afternoon, a barrel that is 400 rounds further along its life than when the load was developed. Each departure is small. Their sum is the miss.

These departures can't be separated after the fact unless they were recorded at the moment of the shot. A hit rate tells you how often you were right. It can't tell you why you were wrong. That takes a record rich enough to attribute the error, and with measured velocity on every shot, Bullseye knows the velocity of the shot that only misses low during these conditions.

What the record makes possible

Separating the error sources

With prediction, conditions and outcome stored together, a miss becomes a residual. Vertical misses that track measured velocity point to the ammunition. Vertical misses that track temperature point to powder sensitivity. Horizontal misses that grow with time of flight and disappear when the wind came from a meter point to the wind call.

Wind or velocity

Wind drift depends on how much the bullet slows down, so a velocity that departs from the prediction carries into the wind hold too. With measured velocity on every shot, a horizontal miss can be divided into what the wind did and what the velocity did.

Truing from all your shots

Conventional truing adjusts MV and drag from one session at one or two distances. A log of fully described shots lets the same correction be estimated across many sessions, distances and atmospheres.

Velocity as a variable

Each shot's measured velocity sits next to its ammo temperature and barrel round count, so temperature sensitivity and the slow loss of speed as the throat erodes build up as a byproduct of ordinary shooting.

Barrel life

Every shot belongs to a rifle, a load and a barrel at a known round count. Changes in dispersion, velocity or first-round behavior can be placed against the round count where they appeared.

Honest statistics

Every input records where it came from. Shots fired on estimated conditions and shots fired on measured conditions can be analyzed separately, so data of uneven quality never quietly weakens a conclusion.

Analysis built on the record

Bullseye Ballistics AI (Sagitta) works directly on your shot log. Ask how much of your horizontal dispersion at 1000 yards is really velocity rather than wind, which wind directions you consistently misread at a given range, or whether your cold-bore shot still lands where it did 500 rounds ago. The answer is built from your own shots, and every number comes from Bullseye's own deterministic solver and statistics.
