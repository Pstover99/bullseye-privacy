Open ecosystems

Open vs. closed ecosystems

Bullseye Ballistics was built from the ground up on a modern, open architecture that welcomes any manufacturer ready to integrate. Your gear, your choice.

Closed

A closed ecosystem is one where the company controls every link in the chain. Its software only talks to its own hardware, its data formats are proprietary, and any third-party product either has to pay to get in or can’t get in at all.

If you want the full experience, you buy everything from one vendor. If you want to swap one piece for something better, you can’t, or you lose features when you do.

Open

An open ecosystem supports common standards and invites compatibility so other products can plug in. Your rangefinder, weather meter, display and solver don’t all have to come from the same company.

Each piece has to earn its spot on merit. If something better comes out next year, you replace that one piece and keep everything else.

The “quality control” argument doesn’t hold up

The usual defense of a closed system sounds reasonable at first:

“We control every part so we can guarantee accuracy and a seamless experience.”

The problem is that opening the door to integrations doesn’t mean giving up control of your own products. A company can still:

  • Hold its own solver, sensors and firmware to the same strict standard
  • Validate and certify specific third-party devices if it wants to
  • Publish a spec for how data has to be formatted and reject anything that doesn’t meet it
  • Clearly label what’s first-party and what isn’t, so nobody confuses one with the other

Quality control applies to what you build. It doesn’t require stopping other people from building alongside you. Every major open platform in tech works this way: the owner keeps tight control of its core product and lets the market compete on everything around it.

So “quality control” is really a reason to test integrations, not a reason to ban them.

The quieter version

We can’t be responsible for other people’s equipment. That’s true, and nobody is asking them to be. A clear support boundary (“we support our products; third-party devices are supported by their makers”) handles it. It’s how almost every other industry works.

Speed of innovation

This is where the gap is widest right now. Sensors, displays, connectivity and software are all moving fast. When a closed ecosystem wants to add a capability, it has to design, build, test and release it itself, on its own schedule and budget. Users wait for whatever the company decides to prioritize.

An open ecosystem gets better through many companies working in parallel. When a new rangefinder, wind meter or display hits the market, it can be useful right away. Nobody has to wait for one company to get around to supporting it or build its own version. The best ideas win because they get adopted, not because one company’s roadmap allowed them.

Closed ecosystem’s ceiling

One company’s engineering capacity.

Open ecosystem’s ceiling

The whole industry’s.

Who actually benefits?

Look at what a closed ecosystem does in practice:

  1. Locks you in Once you’ve bought into one piece, the next purchase is effectively decided for you.
  2. Removes price competition With no alternatives that plug in, there’s no pressure to keep prices fair.
  3. Raises switching costs Your gear, data and habits are all tied to one vendor, so leaving is expensive and painful.
  4. Controls the pace You get new features when the company decides to ship them, not when the technology is ready.

Each of those is a benefit to the company. Recurring revenue, protected margins and a captive customer base are real business advantages, and that’s the real reason most closed ecosystems stay closed.

To be fair

The consumer does get something: a single point of support and a setup that’s known to work together out of the box. But an open ecosystem can offer that too, through certified-compatible devices or a recommended bundle, without forcing the choice.

The open approach keeps that convenience and adds freedom to choose. The closed approach trades the consumer’s freedom for the company’s security.

Bottom line

A closed ecosystem asks customers to trust that one company will always build the best version of every piece. An open ecosystem lets the best piece win, whoever made it.

Companies that are confident in their products don’t need to lock competitors out. They let the products compete.
